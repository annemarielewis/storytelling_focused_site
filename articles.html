Etched into the Landscape

Profile — 2018 | Ken Salazar

A profile of former U.S. Secretary of the Interior Ken Salazar exploring how his family’s centuries-old roots in the American Southwest complicate the immigration debate—and what their story reveals about perseverance, belonging, and the American Dream in the wake of Trump's election.

The story of former Secretary of the Interior Ken Salazar’s heritage

On a sunny Wednesday morning, Ken Salazar, the former Democratic United States senator from Colorado, sits in his office on the 26th floor of the 17th Street Plaza building overlooking the city of Denver. Two large glass windows dominate the room, framing the snow-capped Rocky Mountains to the west and the flat, sprawling city of Denver to the north. On a shelf behind him, trophies awarded for his environmental work as Secretary of the Interior under President Barack Obama sit alongside framed photographs of his children and grandchildren. To his right, photographs of the Obama family hang neatly against a white wall.

I sit across from the former Attorney General, Senator, and Secretary of the Interior with a recorder in hand. I take a deep breath. I adjust the mic gain, and we are ready.

Senator Salazar has agreed to speak about President Trump’s policy goals surrounding Mexican-American immigration in light of his own family history—which carries particular weight when considering how deeply Salazar’s heritage is rooted in the enduring tensions between the U.S. and Mexico. His family has lived in the Southwestern United States since it was still part of colonial New Spain, and as a Hispano, Salazar’s ancestry offers a unique perspective on the current Mexican-American immigration debate.

He thanks me for my interest in his perspective and begins:

“On the questions of the wall between the United States and Mexico, I think it is a shameful initiative by the President of the United States and others who support that effort, and I think it’s partly based on the misconception between the reality of the history of our country and the relationship with Mexico and mis-assumptions that are made about that relationship and that history.”

By this, Senator Salazar means:

“There are many people in this country who are anti-immigrant, who look at Mexicans and Mexican-Americans as recent arrivals into this country, but the fact is that people like my family have been here for multiple generations and were here long before any of the Southwest was part of the United States.”

In the late 1500s, Spain founded the city of Santa Fe, New Mexico. Around this time, the Senator’s family came to live in what is now northern New Mexico. The Southwest was part of New Spain for more than two centuries before Mexico won its independence in 1821. Then, in 1848, after the Mexican-American War, vast stretches of what are now Arizona, Utah, Nevada, California, Colorado, Wyoming, and New Mexico became part of the United States. Mexican families faced a hard choice: immigrate back to Mexico or become U.S. citizens. Most remained tied to their land on what was now U.S. soil, as Senator Salazar’s family did.

After the Mexican-American War, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo addressed citizenship and land rights for Mexicans living in the newly acquired territories, but the “annexed Mexicans became a conquered people living in the Southwest,” Salazar says.

This historical context is important to consider because, as he explains:

“The story for Mexican-Americans in the Southwest is interwoven with the reality of what transpired from the time of the Mexican-American War all the way to the current time. They found themselves as people who had been conquered, living in the Southwestern part of the United States and struggling in many ways for survival, as my grandmother and my grandfather did, and serving as my parents did.”

Salazar’s grandparents were Antonia Cantu and Juan Bautista Salazar. He speaks about them with intense respect and admiration. Throughout their lives, Antonia and Juan Salazar had little wealth or resources, but within their struggle, Salazar finds something extraordinary:

“What I find so inspiring about her and my grandparents is that against all odds, they were able to keep going with great faith that, somehow, the life that they were living would create a better life for their children… And they lived a very hard life.”

But they never lost hope, despite one of the greatest devastations imaginable: losing five of their eight children.

The couple lost their first child while trying to carve a path for their future by homesteading 160 acres of remote land in Colorado. Their five-year-old son, Willy, became extremely ill. In order to acquire title to the land under the Homestead Act, they had to make improvements to it, and so, on the day Willy became ill in 1920, Juan was laboring to fence the property.

That night, Willy began screaming in agony: “Ay mama, ay papa, ay dios!”—Oh mom, oh dad, oh God.

The next day, they put him in a horse-drawn wagon and set out for the nearest doctor. On the way, he continued crying out: “Ay mama, ay papa, ay dios!”

A mile before they reached the doctor’s office, Antonia held Willy as he opened his big blue eyes and said one more time, “Ay mama, ay papa, ay dios,” and died in her arms.

Talking about his grandmother Antonia, Senator Salazar’s tone becomes tender and nostalgic.

“Through her history, I learned a lot about her life and the struggles she had been through.”

Antonia was born May 10, 1884, and passed away in 1969, when Senator Salazar was 14 years old. During those beginning years of Salazar’s life, she lived with him and his parents on the same ranch where she had been born 85 years earlier.

Looking past me, out the window at the sprawling city of Denver, Salazar reminisces:

“I often thought about what it was that kept her going… I would always think to myself: what is it that this wonderful strong woman had about her that kept her going, that gave her faith for the future? What kept her going through the 20s, through the depression of the 30s, through the tough times in the nation: World War Two, The Korean War, and so on?

“My answer is that she had strong faith that her surviving children…my father and two sisters…would somehow have a better life than that, a life with improved conditions than the ones she had…

“My [own] parents raised eight children who all became college graduates, and I know that when my uncle Willy was dying in her arms, she could not have foreseen the fact that many years later, all of her surviving son’s children would graduate from college, that one of them would become a member of the U.S. House of Representatives, the others would become teachers and doctors, and a whole host of other things, and I, as one of the sons, would become a United States Senator and the Secretary of the Interior, and that faith about the future is a faith that I think of as enduring, and that I hope can be recaptured and re-instilled into the story of America.”

Understanding the Southwest’s history, and families like the Salazars, introduces real nuance into an “Us vs. Them” American mentality.

Senator Salazar notes:

“I think, since the immigration debate has been such a divisive issue over the last 30 years, there is a lack of understanding of that history, and I hope that my grandparents’ story, in some ways, elucidates a rich history and one which tells a story about people who have been rooted in the landscapes for centuries that [are] now the United States.”

Knowing the Southwest’s history, and walking down the colorful streets of Santa Fe, New Mexico, with chili peppers festooned on nearly every lamppost, one understands that the Spanish conversations, highway names, and food are not recent arrivals or a takeover of American culture, but the products of centuries of history and a deep and powerful merging of cultures.

The contributions and sacrifices of Mexican-American citizens need to be understood and appreciated.

“What makes sense as a society, as a nation, is to elevate the understanding of the history and contributions of Mexican-Americans in the Southwest… Everybody’s history needs to have a sense of being, because that’s who we are.”

There is a short pause before he slowly paints a nostalgic and warm picture:

“As you look out the front steps of my family’s house in the San Luis Valley, you see the mountains that are named after those who have settled the Southwest. The Sangre De Cristo Mountains to the East, the San Juan mountains on the West. The rivers are named Spanish names, such as the Rio Grande, the San Juan, the San Antonio River, so the history of our ancestors is etched into the landscape of the Southwest, and that’s true for Texas, New Mexico, California, Arizona, Nevada, Colorado, and all of what is now the Southwestern part of the United States.”

Salazar also describes the Trump administration’s proposal to build a wall between the United States and Mexico as arising, in part, from a misunderstanding of the “push-pull of the economics between the United States and Mexico.”

Throughout U.S. history, the country has at times incentivized Mexican immigration for economic reasons.

“In World War Two and the Korean War, for agriculture and a whole host of other things, there has been a pull from the U.S. for these people to come here. Then, when the economy has gotten bad, there is a policy decision to try to push them out. So we’ve had a very broken system of immigration.”

In other words, Mexican workers have at times been encouraged to enter the United States, establishing roots and families, only to face pressure to leave as political and economic conditions changed.

Salazar sees contemporary undocumented immigration as an evolving “economic reality” that is also frequently misunderstood.

“There are huge economic benefits that these undocumented workers are providing to the people here in this country… These are people who are working and contributing in a major way to the economy of the United States.”

He continues:

“The demographic statistics have shown that illegal immigration across the border is significantly down from what it was in the 1990s and before… so the real question to me is how do we as a society treat these people in a humane fashion and not create an underclass of people who live in the shadows of our society, and that is in fact what has happened.”

For a moment, both Salazar and I pause. I take the opportunity to read the inscription on one of the impressive trophies behind him, and I begin to think about his grandmother.

“What would she say if she could know all that you have accomplished? All that her pain, perseverance, and strength had allowed?”

There is a moment of silence before Senator Salazar answers:

“She would give great accolades to the values that have helped my family achieve what they have achieved. Values of hard work, education, love for community, love for the land: the place from where we come, and a sense of adventure, determination, and perseverance.”

My next question carries a lot of weight, so I phrase it in my head before asking:

“And what do you think your success says of the American dream?”

There is another pause.

“I think the American dream is alive and well, but I think that we have leaders in the United States today that are not very helpful of keeping the American dream alive. If you start from the basic premise that every child, no matter their gender, their race, their ethnicity, their background, their economic circumstance, has a god-given ability to become somebody great… and everybody can be great in different ways... and if the right environment is created around a person, then the person will flower to their greatest potential.”

“But if that environment isn’t created, then it’s hard to expect that everyone born into poverty, abuse, or other adversities will be able to persevere through the trauma?”

“I think you summed it up well, and I think the government has a responsibility, and as an expression of the common good, to make sure that the conditions around a child allow that opportunity to be created for that person. And it goes beyond a child, it goes to everybody… Everybody has to be understood of having the human spirit that I think transcends everything, and its humans that put the limitation on that spirit, and we as humans ought not to be supporting governments, in my view, that diminishes the human spirit.”

And that is the end of our interview.

I exhale, and I am thinking of Antonia Cantu Salazar.

We pose for a picture in front of the long windows overlooking Denver to the north and the Rocky Mountains to the west. His secretary, Velina, snaps the photo. Afterward, we pause and look toward the horizon, where a yellow haze dominates.

It’s ground-level smog from industrialization.

I hope the camera didn’t pick it up.

It probably did, but that’s not the point.

The point is that from our view on the 26th floor of the 17th Street Plaza building, we can see the consequences of American development—both the progress and the sacrifices—and what are we to say?

The smog is an allegory. It sums up our conversation.

There has been immense pain in U.S. development and breakthrough, but there has also been immense beauty and perseverance in the human spirit.

What are we to say of progress and sacrifice?

Girl Code Is Hurting Women

Under Review — NYT Opinion

Opinion — October 2027 | An Unpopular Opinion

An essay on the unwritten rules women hold one another to in love and friendship, and on the difference between being hurt and being wronged. Written in October 2027, it is currently under review by The New York Times for its Opinion section.

I was watching the newest episode of my favorite show, Ted Lasso, when a storyline struck me as almost comically unrealistic.

When Roy wants to get back together with Keeley, his on-again, off-again love, she tells him to date other women first, to make sure he isn’t just putting her on a pedestal.

So he does. And, plot twist: he finds a girlfriend. Keeley is, unsurprisingly, devastated and jealous.

Eventually, the new girlfriend realizes Roy is still in love with Keeley. She points this out for his own edification while graciously stepping aside: “You deserve to be with the woman you love.”

She then asks why Roy pursued her if he was still in love with Keeley.

Because, Roy explains, Keeley told him to date other women.

She is immediately and completely understanding, even acting as a quasi-therapist for Roy, helping him understand his own emotions. More than that, she immediately proceeds to adore Keeley, going out of her way to help her for no other reason than: “Anything for Keeley!”

I’m sorry. What woman is this?

Not because I think that kindness is impossible.

But because I’ve been on both sides of this equation, and it is hellishly hard to be either Keeley or the other woman.

Two years ago, one of my closest friends briefly dated a man for about two months. A year after they broke up, I realized I had developed feelings for him. She had previously, out of the blue, told me that if this ever happened, because he and I were also friends, she would want us to be happy. I was confused why she'd say this, as she still had feelings for him, and at the time, I'd found the idea laughable. Though I appreciated the grace in it.

Despite her "pre-blessing," I still felt terrible and even physically sick, as though my brain had committed an ethical violation without consulting me.

When I told her, I bawled my eyes out.

Her response stunned me. She immediately said she was genuinely happy for us. She ardently encouraged me to pursue the relationship and shared that she thought we were a great match.

I checked repeatedly. Was she sure? Was she really OK?

Yes. Every time.

So I believed her, though my internal guilt never lessened.

A few months later, she happily started dating a new guy.

A few months after that, she abruptly stopped responding to my calls, texts, and even inquiries trying to figure out if she was hurt. I asked mutual friends if she was okay; they said she was fine. Eventually, after months of reaching out and confusion, I pressed her to tell me whether my continued attempts to contact her were themselves violating a boundary I didn’t know existed.

She responded that I had betrayed her. She had, in all reality, been entirely devastated. We “had made a fool of her.” I had damaged her ability to trust people. Our friendship was over.

I later learned that while assuring me she supported the relationship, she had been expressing her anguish to mutual friends.

I was blindsided. I felt horrible that she had been in pain, but angry that she had repeatedly told me the opposite of what she felt, then held me responsible for believing her.

But watching Ted Lasso, I realized something uncomfortable about my anger.

Part of it is guilt.

Some part of me thinks I should have known.

Her blessing had been so enthusiastic, so easy. Maybe some part of me recognized what bothered me about the television scene: Come on. What woman is this?

Maybe I chose to believe her words because I wanted them to be true.

That doesn't make me responsible for feelings she didn't communicate, or her responsible for my choices. It means something less satisfying: We were two women navigating love, jealousy, friendship, guilt and autonomy inside a culture that had already told us what all of it was supposed to mean.

Because women have been handed a code.

Don’t date someone your friend dated. Don’t pursue someone your friend likes. Don’t touch what another woman has already emotionally claimed.

Girl code.

We treat these rules as evidence of the depth of female friendship: Men come and go; girlfriends are forever. A good woman would never choose a man over another woman.

And I had failed girl code.

An irrevocable no-no. No ifs, ands, or buts–I should have known not to be with him.

And I have horrible guilt for that–even still, it consumes me.

But my rational self has started to wonder if we have it backward.

I know a group of men who are close friends, several of whom have dated the same women at different times. I’ve heard women remark that these men must not understand real friendship. Real friends wouldn't date the same women.

So I once asked two of them, separately, how they felt about having dated the same woman.

They shrugged.

If she wasn't right for one of them but was right for the other, they reasoned, why should their friendship prevent it? One put it simply: “It’s the girl’s choice. We can both present ourselves, and it’s up to her free will and autonomy. It’s not something between us guys. It’s individually between us and her.”

I was… blown away.

Imagine what imposing the equivalent of girl-code on these aforementioned men would mean for women.

A group of male friends meets a woman. One announces that he likes her first. Everyone else must extinguish whatever feelings they have for her.

Or perhaps she dates one of them. It doesn't work out. Later, she and his friend fall for each other.

Too bad.

Someone else in the group had her first. Or perhaps still likes her.

Her romantic availability is now being determined by agreements among men.

Put that way, the problem becomes obvious.

Yet women impose a remarkably similar system on one another. And while that can objectify the men involved, I’m less interested in defending men than in asking what it does to women.

Because, to be honest, I am not as evolved as the aforementioned men. If I were them, I would surely have big, complicated feelings.

Their lack of possessiveness made me wonder how many of those feelings are inevitable, and how many we have been culturally taught to interpret in a particular damning way through the false feministic, yay-sisterhood rhetoric of “a good girl would never…”

When we define loving female friendship as You will never touch what I have touched; you will never want what I want, ordinary human emotions become moral offenses.

And then, of course, we feel justifiably betrayed when someone commits them.

The pain isn't ridiculous or surprising. It is the logical consequence of the moral framework we've been given. If I believe a loving friend would never date someone I once loved, then when she does, I don't merely experience heartbreak or jealousy.

I experience wrongdoing.

Someone has done something to me, and we hurt much worse than we otherwise would–because now, we don’t just lose a crush or an idea of something; we lose a friend.

I learned the horror of that feeling over a decade and a half ago.

Freshman year of high school, I had a massive crush on a boy. My best friend, Zoey, apparently did too. But she never told me. Until, one day, they showed up at school dating.

I was crushed.

But what devastated me wasn't that he liked Zoey.

It was that Zoey had betrayed me.

I hated her. Really hated her.

Granted, we were high-school freshmen, children walking around with spectacularly unfinished brains. But there was also something deeper going on: Zoey was also trapped by the rule I was using to condemn her.

It’d take immense bravery to tell me she liked him too when my own feelings had already transformed hers into a moral offense, culturally. She had been cast as the villain in the story before she'd done anything except like the same boy I liked. And she knew this. Just as I would, a decade and a half later, also know this.

I moved away the following year. When I returned junior year, they had broken up. And, slowly, Zoey and I became friends again.

Then best friends again.

And, to this day, we are close friends.

The boy came up in conversation a few years ago, and we burst out laughing.

Because this is what our story became: When Zoey received a scholarship to trek Everest in Nepal, I threw her a surprise party for the ages and became close to her mother in the process. When I endured bullying and abuse, Zoey was the person I looked to for guidance. When her mother died two years after high school, I was the first one there. And when I lose my own mother someday, Zoey will be the first person I call.

We grew up together, suffered and grieved together, and there's a powerful bond in that.

It would have been preposterous and tragic for our story to end with a boy: for her to end at "girl villain.”

There is a distinction we don't make often enough between causing someone pain and wronging them.

Other people’s autonomy can hurt us. Someone can stop loving us. Someone we love can love somebody else. A friend can develop feelings for someone we once loved, or perhaps still love.

None of that makes the pain imaginary.

But pain does not automatically establish wrongdoing. And friendship cannot reasonably require another person to surrender every competing emotion or life decision simply because ours arrived first.

What if female love and loyalty meant something harder?

What if loving another woman meant recognizing that she is every bit as complicated and as involuntary in many of her emotions as you are, and entitled to them?

What if we could say:

This hurts me terribly.

And I don't think you're terrible for it.

Those truths can coexist.

This doesn't make every romantic situation among friends ethically uncomplicated. Deception matters. Cheating matters. Cruelty matters. Manipulation matters. Selfishness matters. And friends are also allowed to decide that some situations, even understandable ones, are simply too painful to remain close through.

But having feelings is not a betrayal. And another person's autonomy does not become one simply because it hurts us.

Which brings me back to Ted Lasso and why I don’t believe that scene: not because Roy’s girlfriend is generous toward Roy and Keeley. What I don’t believe is how easy it is in the culture we exist in.

She has just discovered that a man she genuinely likes is still in love with his ex. She has lost something, yet is instantly fine and sees Keeley not as a rival but instead as her inspiration.

The ease of stoicism, sisterhood and kindness after a storm, in the culture we exist in, that’s the fantastical part.

Kindness isn’t the unrealistic part. Numbness is.

We are not robots, so the aspiration isn't to become so evolved that nothing hurts or pisses us off. Loving our sisters doesn't anesthetize us against hurt, jealousy or loss.

The aspiration is something both less glamorous and more difficult: To hurt without requiring a villain. To be jealous and hurt without mistaking pain for evidence that someone has wronged us. To lose, and still choose to like and respect Keeley as a fellow flawed woman, also deserving of love.

My friendship with Zoey survived past jealousy because eventually we became bigger than the rules we'd inherited. We chose the friendship over the claim.

Sometimes another person’s freedom and autonomy will wound us simply because we are all trying to find our happiness at once.

That’s part of loving people who are free.

But it doesn’t need to end at the wound.

What if women could hurt, be jealous, grieve, and still refuse to make enemies of one another?

Maybe that’s a better girl code.

Love your girlfriends enough to let them be human.

Love yourself enough to survive when they are.

And even as I write this, I know I don’t live up to such ideals. I imagine a friend eventually dating the love of my life, and I instinctively want them to burst into flames.

It’s a pursuit to shed a cultural belief bred from our worth being defined by men, unrealistic beauty standards, our human need for security, et cetera. But it’s a shedding I feel is healthier and will ultimately make us happier and in deeper sisterhood.

Advice from the Oldest Screenwriters Still Alive

Feature — 2017 | WeScreenplay

A 2017 piece published by WeScreenplay when I was 20, gathering advice from some of Hollywood’s longest-working screenwriters and directors, on everything from adapting novels to finding one’s voice.

The average age of a screenwriter working on a top-grossing Hollywood film is around 46 years old. However, many of the most decorated and iconic screenwriters and directors far surpass this bracket—and they have a lot of wisdom to offer upcoming generations of screenwriters.

In this article, we’ll hear advice and take tips from some of Hollywood’s longest-working and most iconic male and female screenwriters and directors. James Ivory, Francis Ford Coppola, Brian De Palma, Steven Spielberg, Emma Thompson, Lucinda Coxon, Leslie Dixon, and Jane Campion share their wisdom on topics ranging from student filmmaking and novel adaptations to finding one’s voice and breaking gender roles.

Number One: James Ivory on Novel Adaptations and the Role of a Screenwriter

James Ivory is an American director and screenwriter born on June 7, 1928, in Berkeley, California. Over the span of his career, he has won dozens of awards, including an Oscar at age 89.

Creative Screenwriting interviewed Ivory in 2017 for the 30th anniversary of the renowned film Maurice, an adaptation of E.M. Forster’s novel, which he directed and co-wrote. When adapting a novel to a screenplay, Ivory advises:

“If you’re going to adapt any novel, you want to stick to it. [...] I believe you have to be true to what the author attempted to say. If you like the author’s tone of voice, it’s a good thing if that tone of voice comes through… you want to preserve that in some way if you can.”

Ivory later adapted the award-winning 2017 film Call Me by Your Name from André Aciman’s 2007 novel. For his work, Ivory won the Academy Award for Best Adapted Screenplay, as well as awards from BAFTA, the Writers Guild, and Critics Choice.

Furthermore, Ivory has some nonconformist advice for directors, like himself, about the role screenwriters should have in the production of a film:

“Unless you yourself are an experienced writer with a gift for dialogue, a gift for deciphering human motivation, and a talent for descriptive scene-setting, trust your writer. Few directors are able to successfully write their own screenplays from scratch. That is, indeed a god-given gift and is extremely rare.”

Oftentimes, directors and producers kick the screenwriter to the curb as soon as the script is done. Writers have little to no power in production past this point, but Ivory cautions against this modus operandi:

“When you have more or less gotten together the film you shot, and can bear to show it to anybody, invite your writer into the editing room so he, or she, can see where [it] went wrong… Directors like myself, who are sometimes too enamored of their work, need to have their eyes opened. Better by the screenwriter than by studio executives and focus groups later on.”

Whether or not Hollywood will heed his advice is a whole other story.

Number Two: Francis Ford Coppola on Trusting One’s Vision, Finding One’s Voice, and Plunging in Broke

Most of us screenwriters know the feeling of rereading a script for the 100th time and absolutely despising it. However, picking apart and “fixing” pieces of our plots in this state may be counterproductive, because our distaste may be a product of needing a break rather than a constructive observation about the state of our work.

Francis Ford Coppola, born on April 7, 1939, has decades of experience in the film industry and multiple Academy Awards and nominations spanning his screenwriting and directing work. Most iconically, he is known for directing The Godfather trilogy.

Addressing self-critique, he noted in a 2011 99U interview:

“I believe that when you write something, when I write something, I turn it over and I don’t look at it. Because I believe the writer, the young writer, has a hormone that makes them hate what they’ve written. And yet, the next morning, when you look at it, you say, ‘Oh that’s not bad.’ But the first second you hate it.”

There you have it: allow your mind and your eyes a chance to readjust and ground themselves before going back in to edit.

Furthering his advice for young writers, Coppola affirms his belief that in the world of art, there are no new ideas devoid of the influence of old ones. We take what has come before us and transform it through our own voices. As Coppola assures young writers:

“We want you to take from us. We want you, at first, to steal from us, because you can’t steal. You will take what we give you and you will put it in your own voice and that’s how you will find your voice. [...] And that’s how you begin. And then one day someone will steal from you.”

A last note from Coppola has to do with plunging into the financially precarious industry of film—not a small risk. Coppola puts it like this:

“Try to disconnect the idea of cinema with the idea of making a living and money. Because there are ways around it. You work another job and get up at five in the morning and write your script.”

Perhaps this view is a little too idealistic, but as Coppola reiterates:

“I always had a good philosophy about risks. The only risk is to waste your life, so that when you die, you say, Oh, I wish I had done this.”

Number Three: Brian De Palma on Student Filmmaking

Brian De Palma is one of Hollywood’s most successful psychological-horror directors and screenwriters. Among his many famous films, he wrote and directed Raising Cain and directed Carrie, an adaptation of Stephen King’s novel.

De Palma worked in the film industry for decades, but he originally went to school to study physics before studying film at Sarah Lawrence.

De Palma shares his wisdom for those studying film, a principal point being to cast good actors:

“The biggest mistake in student films is that they are usually cast so badly, with friends and people the directors know. Actually you can cover a lot of bad direction with good acting.”

Furthermore, De Palma advises that it is not enough just to attend film school. Getting internships where the movie magic is happening is key to getting into the industry, because there is only so much you can learn from those outside it:

“The real trouble with film school is that people teaching are so far out of the industry that they don’t give the students an idea of what’s happening.”

To put it simply: get good actors, and surround yourself with industry action no matter what stage of learning you’re at.

Number Four: Steven Spielberg on Escape and Idiosyncrasy

Of course, we need to include Steven Spielberg! As one of Hollywood’s most well-known directors, with decades in the industry and multiple Academy Awards, Emmys, and Golden Globes—not to mention a huge assortment of other awards—he has a great deal of wisdom to share.

One of the beliefs he holds is that storytellers embed pieces of themselves in the stories they tell, even if unconsciously. Nothing comes from nothing; the stories we tell originate either as a product of others sharing theirs or as components of our own lives:

“You can’t intellectually purge yourself of who you are. Whatever that is, it’s going to come out in the wash, the film wash. What you are is going to be relevant, if not to yourself, to the movies you make.”

Embrace the pieces of your own story and write them authentically and anew. And yet, as Spielberg puts it:

“The public has an appetite for anything about imagination, anything that is as far away from reality as is creatively possible.”

A lot of intriguing storytelling is a balancing act between the very real emotional touchpoints we all experience and know to be true, combined with bizarre fantasies and scenarios, personally unknown, that allow us all to momentarily escape our own worlds.

Lastly, idiosyncratic stories are beautiful; a degree of ambiguity allows a viewer to pick out the variations of a theme and meaning that are most needed and relevant to them. However, this too is a balancing act and represents the age-old struggle—and art—of telling just enough to keep everyone in tune with the same emotional touchpoints:

“The most amazing thing for me is that every single person who sees a movie, not necessarily one of my movies, brings a whole set of unique experiences. Now, through careful manipulation and good storytelling, you can get everybody to clap at the same time, to hopefully laugh at the same time, and to be afraid at the same time.”

Number Five: Emma Thompson on Screenwriting Basics

First off, the oldest women screenwriters in Hollywood are significantly younger than the oldest men, reflecting an industry that has traditionally been dominated by men. Keep in mind that our oldest featured female screenwriters here are primarily in their 60s, as opposed to their late 80s and 90s.

Emma Thompson has been involved in the film industry for decades as an actress and screenwriter. She has won an array of prestigious awards, including an Academy Award for Best Adapted Screenplay for Sense and Sensibility. Her screenwriting work also includes Nanny McPhee and Wit.

Keeping momentum going in a plot and avoiding stagnant periods can be more challenging than it at first sounds. Every scene matters, needs to be succinct, and should further the story. Thompson shared her advice on how to accomplish this in a 2015 Screencraft interview:

“There should be an event in every scene. What’s the event?”

If there is no event, physical or psychological, the scene may be unneeded fluff.

Another screenwriting basic Thompson addresses is where to start a plot. At first, it can be hard to put one’s script down on paper, but:

“Start writing, then when you get to the end of it, try throwing out the first three scenes. See what happens.”

Throwing out the first three scenes—or reordering scenes—can have an incredibly drastic effect on a plot, resulting in exciting twists and turns and intriguing juxtapositions the writer wouldn’t have considered without experimenting with the cut-and-paste tools.

As an experimental practice, save a first draft, then mix the scenes up a little. You may hate it and choose to ignore it, but then again, you may be inspired by seeing things a little differently.

As a last piece of advice from Thompson, we come to stage directions. Screenwriters are often encouraged to write as little and as succinctly as possible, only enough to get an imperative point across. Much ultimately comes down to the director’s vision and discretion. However, this doesn’t mean a screenwriter should omit stage directions or put little effort into them. As Thompson puts it:

“If the stage directions are very well written, I’m much more likely to take the writer seriously. If the stage directions are lazy or banal, I get bored very quickly. I make my stage directions as funny as I can.”

In short, if you’re a screenwriter and want your vision to be told on screen, craft your stage directions engagingly and thoughtfully. However, a screenwriter’s vision is seldom the final product. As Thompson says:

“Sometimes [the screenwriter is] not at all [involved in the finished product]. Sometimes the screenwriter has been long forgotten and disposed of, possibly even killed and buried.”

Not every screenwriter is so lucky as to have their opinions valued as much as someone like James Ivory recommends.

The best way to ensure your stage directions are followed and your vision is rendered on screen? Be lucky enough to become both the screenwriter and the director.

Number Six: Lucinda Coxon on Truth’s Relativity and Killing Darlings

Lucinda Coxon has been writing for performance for decades. She wrote her first Hollywood screenplay in 2002 and adapted the award-winning 2015 film The Danish Girl from the 2000 novel by David Ebershoff, which was itself inspired by real people.

“Based on a true story” is an interesting phrase in film production.

On the nature of truth, there has long been a philosophical argument that truth in storytelling can be malleable, relative, and based as much on emotion as concrete facts. Stated another way, sometimes the closer one gets to recreating the emotional truth of a situation—even if that means altering physical facts—the truer the story can feel.

Of course, this paradigm has a time and a place. Screenwriter Lucinda Coxon has something to say about this approach as it applies to screenwriting:

“I think any film that is based on life or real events you want it to be accurate but what really matters is that it's truthful. That is not always the same thing. You really want to convey the spirit of the enterprise rather than ticking every single factual box.”

Leaving the realm of truth, Coxon reflects on one of the hardest parts of adapting a book for the screen:

“The hardest part is being brutal about leaving things out... I can let elements sit in the script and inform it for a while, but there’s a moment where you accept that something will never actually be in the film. It’s always a sad relief once you’ve hit ‘delete’.”

This practice is often referred to as “killing one’s darlings”: omitting otherwise great pieces of writing that simply don’t make their best debut in the given plot. It is a skill—or a discipline, really—that all writers of any medium have to learn.

And, of course:

“Even if the work’s a big success, some reviewer, somewhere, will always hate what you’ve done. But you just keep going. Do the next thing. The next thing.”

Retie your shoelaces and trudge on, because that’s the movie business! You are driven and passionate; let the rest be background noise.

Number Seven: Leslie Dixon on Adaptations and Writing “Nasty” as a Woman

Leslie Dixon moved to Hollywood at age 26 with hardly any money and worked her way up through the ranks. After a few years, she got a job as a script reader and learned what devices made a lucrative script versus a flop. Her own screenplays started being purchased by production companies in the 1980s. Over the following decades, Dixon would write Mrs. Doubtfire, Pay It Forward, and Freaky Friday, among many other renowned movies.

On novel adaptations, Dixon advises:

“There are source material that have a great idea, but not a single scene that would translate into a cinematic experience, so you have to change it.”

Stated another way, there are emotions and plot-furthering mechanisms in books that don’t apply directly to the screen. A novel can get inside a character’s head and reveal motivations and desires that, in film, may only be revealed by altering dialogue or actions—or adding a character.

This ties back into what Lucinda Coxon was saying about the importance of emotional truth, as compared to physical truth, in screenplays.

Furthermore, when thinking of adapting a book, one should ask:

“Would I buy a ticket to this? That’s the best jumping off point for writing something. Because for every hundred novels that are written, there’s maybe only one that has a movie screaming to get out.”

Looking forward, an aspiration of Dixon’s was to write more “nasty” screenplays of the kind men were more commonly hired to write. As she put it:

“I want to make sure I can do more pictures like Limitless, and that I am thought of as a chic who can write like a guy and that you would hire for a job that you normally might not hire a woman to do. Nasty, visceral thrillers…”

Breaking into the boys’ club of Hollywood, let alone getting hired to write “nasty thrillers” as a woman, is no small feat.

Number Eight: Jane Campion on Breaking Gender Roles and Telling Stories

Jane Campion has worked in film for decades, becoming one of its most acclaimed female filmmakers. Her career also reflects just how comparatively few women historically reached the highest levels of recognition in directing.

To this, Campion reflects:

“I’ve spent my whole life asking, ‘What are the men thinking?’ and I think it’s time for them to ask, ‘What are the women thinking?’ in a different way than they would have done before.”

One way to change that imbalance is for more women to enter the industry, challenge its structure, and rise. As Campion puts it:

“I would love to see more women directors because they represent half the population—and gave birth to the whole world. Without them writing and being directors, the rest of us are not going to know the whole story.”

The glass ceiling slowly cracks, and if you are a female screenwriter out there, let these featured women be proof that it can be done. That it needs to be done.

On getting film ideas, Campion reflects:

“When you make films, you are telling stories and you try to do things that you personally are trying to discover in the world.”

The stories we most effectively create—or are inspired to retell—often address themes that we are grappling with or ideas that excite us in our own lives.

Creative inspiration can be found in the simplest moments and mundane movements of our own lives.

Pay attention.
